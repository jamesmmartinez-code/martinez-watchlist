# The White-Aspen's Warning

*A fragment, found wedged in the fork of a white-aspen at the forest-edge of
the south Whisperwood, where the path bends toward the cobble. A scrap of
birch-paper folded once and pinned through the fold with a single horsehair,
knotted twice — once at the cinch and once at the tail. The hand is block-cut
and runic, slow the way Stone-Tongue is slow, but written in Common because
the leaves are a Common-tongue lesson. The paper is thin enough that the
fold has gone soft along the crease. The ink has set into the grain on the
warm side of the paper and faded on the cold side, the way old ink will
when a leaf has carried it sideways across many summers. The horsehair is
white. The knots are tied the way a Steppe-rider ties a halter: cinch first,
tail last, both of them small.*

---

## What the leaf says

> *I write this for the road, not for myself. The Stone-Tongue does not
> ask its riders to write. It asks us to stand. But the leaves of the
> white-aspen are not Stone-Tongue's lesson. They are the road's, and
> the road has nothing to write with but riders.*
>
> *So. Listen.*
>
> *The white-aspen carries its leaves the way a courier carries a satchel
> — close on the warm side, loose on the cold. On any ordinary morning the
> wind comes through and the satchel turns and shows you the warm side
> and you go on. The warm side of a white-aspen leaf is the green that
> the sun has spoken to. The cold side is the pale, the silver, the side
> that has not been spoken to and is not asking to be.*
>
> *On the morning before a snow that no one in the valley is yet
> expecting, the white-aspen turns its satchel inside out. The pale
> sides come up. They do not flutter the way they do in a wind; they
> sit. The whole tree goes silver while the rest of the forest stays
> green, and if you are walking the cobble path that bends toward the
> south Whisperwood, you can see it from the place the windmill begins
> to be small behind you.*
>
> *This is not a portent. It is a leaf doing what leaves do. The leaf
> is older than the cold; the cold is older than the rider; the rider
> is older than the saddle. We are all of us in our own ages. The leaf
> is reading the air for itself, and what it is reading is that the
> cold the leaves know first is coming down off the mountain in three
> mornings, sometimes two.*
>
> *The leaves will read the air better than your nose will. Better than
> your bay's ears. Better than the mountain itself, which has too much
> stone in it to feel a small cold. Trust the leaves. The clans call
> this* haerel *— the leaf-reading. It is not a craft. It is a paying
> of attention.*
>
> *You will know you have read a haerel right by what is done after.
> The kettle goes on at the courier-house before you reach the door.
> The saddle is lifted from its peg without your asking. The door is
> left ajar. They will not tell you. The clans never tell the rider
> they have called* haerel-vethen *— leaf-trusted. The mercy of the word
> is that it is not spoken to the face. The rider learns by the kettle.*
>
> *Do not say you saw the leaves first. Do not say it to the village
> elder, do not say it to the smith, do not say it to the road-keeper.
> Say only: the bay was restless. Say only: there is a wind off the
> mountains. The leaves do not need crediting; the leaves are not
> asking. A* haerel *spoken aloud is a haerel asked to make a name for
> itself, and that is not what the leaves are for.*
>
> *I taught a courier once, one Foxthaw, in a hamlet on the lower lip
> of the High Steppe. He was nine and his hands were small. I showed
> him the white-aspen on the lower pass and I said: pale side up, three
> mornings; pale side up and not fluttering, two. I do not know if he
> kept the lesson. I think he did. I think he is keeping it now in a
> place he did not know he would keep it. The road owns its riders the
> way the sky owns its weather — without their consent, and well.*
>
> *I leave this leaf at the forest-edge because the forest-edge is
> where the leaf-reading begins. A rider who has come this far south
> and has not yet seen a white-aspen go silver will see one tonight, or
> next Foxthaw, or the Foxthaw after. The leaf will be waiting. So
> will the* ai-velin *— the long path — for the rider who has been
> taught to read it.*
>
> *I am old now. Older than the boy I taught, and older than the bay
> who carried me here. I will not, I think, ride the south road again.
> I will not see the cobble bend, or the windmill that is small from
> here, or the lantern with the copper coin in its shutter that I am
> told is lit at a Briarwood gate every Sunpetal turn. I am told this
> by riders who have ridden the south road and come back, and I have
> believed them because the lantern is a kind thing for a courier to
> set down at a gate and the boy I taught was a kind boy. But I will
> not see it.*
>
> *So I leave this leaf in the place a leaf would be left. The road
> is mine to* kerrithen *as much as anyone's, and what I am laying
> down is the lesson, not the rider. The lesson stays. The rider goes
> on. This is* kerritha-ai *— laid down for the long path's sake —
> in summer, at the forest-edge, where the cold has not yet come. The
> Longnight has its own kerritha-ai at the windowsill at dawn. The
> forest has this one, here, at a fork in a white-aspen, in the warm
> months when the lesson can still be read by a courier whose hands
> are not yet cold.*
>
> *To the courier who finds this and does not need it: pass it on.
> Press the pale side up against the bark again, fold the paper back
> the way I folded it, pin it with your own horsehair if mine has worn
> through. The white-aspen will keep it for the next rider. That is*
> thirre. *That is the* haerel *the tree itself is doing, on a longer
> turn than ours.*
>
> *To the courier who finds this and needs it: ride. The cold the
> leaves know first is the cold you will be glad you knew about three
> mornings before it came. Bank your ash-pile high. Tighten the door
> against the wind. Set the kettle. Lift the saddle from its peg, even
> if you are not riding tomorrow. The pegs forget. Do not let the
> pegs forget.*
>
> *I do not sign this. The saying is the signing. The horsehair is
> the seal.*
>
> *— a rider of the High Steppe, who learned the leaves before he was
> taught Stone-Tongue and who taught the leaves once, in a place that
> is not on any of the Iron Crown's maps, to a boy he was not, in the
> end, given much time with*

## A scribe's small note, added later

*Pinned to the back of the leaf, in a different hand — a Briarwood
scribe's, the same family whose pond-leaf is kept beside the well and
whose Vigil-leaf is kept in the cedar box at the hearth. The hand is
the eldest sibling's, written, by the look of the strokes, on a
Reapmoon evening with the sun nearly down:*

> *The leaf above mine is older than my grandmother and was found by
> our village's stablemaster the autumn he came to us. He has not said
> so. He returned it to the white-aspen the same Foxthaw he took it
> down to read it. I have copied it here on the second-thinnest paper
> I had, and I have pinned my copy with one of his bay-mare's own white
> tail-hairs. He left the original where he found it. He has said
> nothing. He is reading the leaves, even now, and he will not say
> that either.*
>
> *We do not, in Briarwood, call him* haerel-vethen. *We have no word
> for it in our tongue. But the kettle goes on at the inn and the
> saddle is lifted from the third peg before we know the snow is
> coming, and that is a leaf-trusting we have learned to live by.*
>
> *— a scribe of Briarwood, who has been told by her younger sister
> that the pond-leaves and the cedar-leaves and now this leaf are
> all of one ai-velin, and who is beginning to believe her*

## Author note for downstream agents

This codex page introduces **Yorick as a canonical narrator slot,**
parallel to Halsa (Edda's *haethe* fragment), Caedr, and Reseda. Yorick
is *kerrithen-named* per `lore/npcs/stablemaster_roan.md`: the name
appears in this file's metadata anchors but never inside the leaf's
text. The leaf is signed by hand-style and by the Steppe convention of
the saying-being-the-signing.

Two new Old Faerie words enter canon here, both built around the
Steppe-rider weatherwork that `lore/npcs/stablemaster_roan.md` seeded:

- ***haerel*** — the leaf-reading itself; one *does* haerel.
- ***haerel-vethen*** — the rider the courier-houses have called
  leaf-trusted; never spoken to the rider's face.

The Stone-Tongue cap of three words held by `lore/world.md` is
**intentionally not raised.** This rider is Stone-Tongue, but writes
Common because the leaves are a Common-tongue lesson. Future writers
MUST NOT introduce a fourth Stone-Tongue word in this fragment's
register; if a Stone-Tongue word is needed in a Yorick voice, surface
it in a separate codex page (the cradle codex, the *ostren* codex, or
a future Long-Ride fragment).

The two-hand structure — Yorick's leaf above, the Briarwood scribe's
note below — is the THIRD Briarwood-scribe family voice to surface in
the codex pool, after `data/codex/longnight_vigil.md` (the eldest
sibling's hearth-leaf) and `data/codex/pond_and_lanterns.md` (the
younger sister's pond-leaf). It is the eldest sibling's hand again,
this time at the forest-edge. The sibling-canon now has three leaves
on three of the village's quietest turnings: the pond at Honeysong,
the cedar box at Longnight, and now the white-aspen at Foxthaw. The
fragment's closing paragraph ("told by her younger sister that the
pond-leaves and the cedar-leaves and now this leaf are all of one
*ai-velin*") closes a canonical triangle the Briarwood scribal family
has been building for years; Builder may flag
`briarwood_scribe_triangle_closed` once the player has read all three
fragments, with no in-game UI surface required (the achievement is
the reading).

The fragment **does not name Cailen** and **does not confirm the boy
is Roan.** Both are Withholding-Ledger silences per
`lore/npcs/stablemaster_roan.md`. The leaf gestures at "a courier I
taught one Foxthaw, in a hamlet on the lower lip of the High Steppe"
— that is the entire surfacing. Future writers MUST keep the silence;
the Briarwood scribe's note (in the SECOND hand) is the closest the
codex comes to identifying the boy, and even there she identifies him
only by what he does in the present: he is the man who returned the
original leaf to the white-aspen the same Foxthaw he took it down to
read it.
