# Vellum's Spine — A Mason's Leaf

*A fragment, found in the foundation course of the Briarwood well — slipped
on its narrow side into a gap no hand-span ever found, between the
keeping-stone and the second course, and waxed at the corners against the
seep. The well had to come up two seasons in a row before the leaf was
turned out, and even then it was the mason's apprentice — not the mason —
who saw the wax-shine catch the lantern. The hand is squared, the
line-edges are aligned, the diacritics are chisel-marks rather than
pen-strokes. The hand is not the scribal family's. It belongs to a
different Briarwood line — the line of the wall-cutters, the
keeping-stone setters, the people who put themselves under what the
village stands on and do not, as a rule, sign their work.*

---

## What the leaf says

> *I am writing this so the next mason finds it. If you have come to take
> the well up, set it down again the way you would set down a friend's
> blade — slowly, on stone, and not in the dirt. The keeping-stone is
> the* ***vellath.*** *It does not have to be the prettiest. It has to be
> the truest. Test it twice. Tap with the knuckle, not the chisel. If
> the sound is hollow, the wall will outlive you and the well will not.*
>
> *I am writing this also because I have been told, by my father who was
> told by his, that masons should write down what masons know. We have
> not been a writing line. We have been a setting line. But three
> winters ago a stone in the south wall of Bram's grandmother's inn
> came up out of true, and the wall was not ours, and we found that we
> could not say to the next mason* what *had gone wrong, only that
> something had. So I am setting this down. The next mason will not
> have to learn it twice.*
>
> *Here is what masons in this valley know. None of it is secret. All of
> it is slow.*
>
> ***The vellath.*** *The first stone of the course. The keeping-stone.
> It carries every other stone above it; it carries also the road that
> runs past the wall, and the children who run on the road, and the
> weather that decides whether the children will run there in
> Greenshield or in Wolfwake. A vellath set wrong is a vow sworn
> wrong. Set it twice. Tap it twice. If you doubt it on the second
> tap, lift it out, turn it, and try it again. Vellum is patient. So
> are you.*
>
> ***The thol-ennen.*** *The slow word. It is what a mason says under
> the breath when a stone has gone in true and the mortar will not be
> needed twice. I will not write the words my father taught me; words
> said under the breath are private. I will only say that the word is
> the same word that goes onto a wedding-ring without being spoken
> aloud, and that masons who marry late in life — there are a few —
> tend to set their best work in the first year of the marriage, for
> a reason that is not coincidence and not magic and is, I think,
> simply the* thol-ennen *being said twice in the same season.*
>
> ***The caer-vellis.*** *The spine that holds. We use this for walls
> that have stood three generations without cracking. We use it also
> for a strong back at the end of a long day, and for the mountain
> ring that frames the valley. The mountain ring is not a wall. It
> is the other one. The one Vellum laid down when he broke. It is
> the* caer-vellis *of the world. Some of the older folk say his
> spine is set the same way our walls are: keeping-stone first,
> course by course, the topmost course not yet laid. I do not know
> if this is true. I know that when I set a vellath I am setting it
> in the same direction the mountain ring runs, and that the few
> times I have set one across the grain — for a doorframe, against
> the wall — the work has wanted twice the mortar and twice the
> tap. The world prefers its own grain. We work along it.*
>
> *About Vellum I will say what masons say to apprentices on their
> first lift, and no more. He is patient. He broke against the other
> one and was set down; the spine of the world is what was set down.
> He keeps memory the way a wall keeps weather — not by resisting,
> only by being there for it, course on course, until the weather
> has gone elsewhere. Walls are slow. Vellum is slow. Masons are not
> slow but learn to be.*
>
> *About the other one I will say nothing. The smith-line knows that
> name better than we do, and they do not say it either. We do not
> call.*
>
> *Three rules I have written for the next mason. Read them in this
> order. Do not change the order.*
>
> ***1. The vellath is the vow.*** *Set it twice. The course above it
> trusts the course below.*
>
> ***2. The thol-ennen is private.*** *Say it under the breath. Say it
> when the work is true. Do not say it for show, do not say it to a
> visitor, do not say it twice in the same hour. The slow word is
> slow.*
>
> ***3. The caer-vellis is shared.*** *No mason owns a wall. The
> wall belongs to the village, and to the next mason, and to the
> children who will not learn your name. Sign nothing. Set the
> keeping-stone true. Walk away. Vellum saw.*
>
> *Last: when a wall comes up that you laid — and walls do come up,
> in seven generations or in two — do not be wounded. The* vellath
> *will go back into the next course unless it has cracked, and even
> if it has cracked, the* thirre *of it stays in the place. The
> place remembers being held. Set the next vellath where the old one
> was, if the wall is rising in the same line. The work is patient.
> It does not need to be young.*
>
> ***Kerritha*** *the chisel at the threshold of your last work. Not
> in the forge. Not in the river. At the door of the wall you set
> best. The wall will keep the asking.*
>
> ***Ai-velin*** *to whoever reads this. Walk warmly. Walk slowly.
> Tap twice.*
>
> — *signed only with a mason's mark: a vertical line under a
> horizontal line, the spine under the lintel. The wax has held the
> ink against the seep for longer than the writer expected.*

## A second hand, found folded inside the first

*(A smaller leaf, tucked inside the fold of the first. The hand is the
mason's daughter's. The diacritics are softer; the chisel-cadence is
gone; the writer has been to the south-country once and has come back.)*

> *My father set the keeping-stone of the well in the third year of
> his last marriage. He said the* thol-ennen *under his breath, and
> my mother heard it from the kitchen window because the wind was
> wrong that morning, and she did not say she had heard it because
> the slow word is private. She told me on the night before she
> died, and I am writing it down so my own daughter has it without
> having to wait that long.*
>
> *The well has not run dry in nineteen winters.*
>
> *I am leaving the leaf where my father left it. The next mason will
> find it. I will not be a mason. I am an inn-keeper's daughter who
> married a mason's son, and the mason-line passes through me but
> does not stop in me, and that is the right shape for it. My
> daughter taps stones in her sleep. We will see.*
>
> — *the daughter*

## What this establishes

This fragment is the **first codex page authored from outside the
scribal family arc** (sister / brother / cousin / grandfather / Maeve,
per `data/codex/thiars_mercy_owed_to_prey.md` author note). The hand
is a Briarwood **mason's**, in a different cadence — squared, chiseled,
oath-shaped. The villagers, in canon, can tell the two hands apart on
sight: the scribal family's diacritics sweep; the mason's diacritics
chip. Future Lorekeeper runs may extend this *second* family with one
or two more leaves before introducing a third Briarwood hand. Variety
is canon.

Concretely the leaf gives Briarwood three customs that downstream
agents may now lean on:

1. **The vellath is the vow.** The first stone of any wall in
   Briarwood is the *keeping-stone* and is canonically called *the
   vellath.* Masons test it twice with the knuckle. The custom anchors
   the world.md line *"His name is sworn into walls and into
   wedding-rings"* — the wall's keeping-stone is Vellum's name made
   physical, set down rather than spoken. This applies to *all*
   Briarwood walls in canon: the well, the smithy, Bram's inn, the
   mountain-ring's foothill terraces, the eastern gate. Future
   Environment passes that place new walls in Briarwood may light a
   single foundation stone slightly warmer than the rest of the
   course; that stone is the *vellath.* No interaction required. The
   stone is just *there.*

2. **The thol-ennen is private.** Masons say the *slow word* under
   the breath when a stone has gone in true. The leaf is explicit
   that no NPC will say it aloud, no quest will *teach* it to the
   player, and no UI will surface it. This is a deliberate
   *un-monetized* canon: the player will never collect *thol-ennen*
   as a quest item or a renown bump. The word lives in the silence
   between a mason and their work. (Cross-canon: the same shape of
   privacy that the *bhren-aïth* takes in `thiars_mercy_owed_to_prey.md` —
   the courtesy is not for the renown system. Eldoria's gods honor
   silence.) The word has one further canonical use: a *thol-ennen*
   is the word that goes onto a wedding-ring without being spoken
   aloud. Briarwood weddings have, on this canon, no spoken vow;
   the ring is the vow, and the mason's word is the ring's word.
   Two of the four Wardens (per `wardens_of_the_mark.md`) have set
   their own *thol-ennen*; future NPC bibles may quietly reflect
   this without naming it.

3. **The caer-vellis is shared.** The *spine that holds* is the canon
   word for the mountain ring (Vellum's spine, per `world.md`), and
   by extension for any Briarwood wall that has stood three
   generations or longer. The grammar of the word is intentionally a
   *holding* compound, not a *boasting* one — the spine does not
   announce itself. This rules a small but load-bearing piece of
   architectural canon: **no Briarwood mason signs their work.**
   Masons' marks on stones are *placement marks* (a vertical line
   under a horizontal — the spine under the lintel) and are not
   names. The mason in this leaf is, by canon, **unnamable** — not
   because the village has forgotten her, but because she did not
   leave a name. The leaf is signed only by the mark. This is canon.
   Future quests that involve Briarwood masonry must respect this:
   no NPC in the village knows the well-mason's name, and no quest
   may *reveal* it. The wall belongs to the village.

The leaf also gives Vellum a further canonical posture, beyond what
`world.md` set: **Vellum keeps memory by being there for weather, not
by resisting it.** This is the patient-stone reading made explicit.
A wall does not push back against rain; it stands, course on course,
until the rain has gone elsewhere. Future writers who give Vellum a
*resistance* posture — a vengeance-god, a battlements-god, a god of
walls-as-warding — break this canon. Vellum is *with* the weather,
not against it. The mountain ring is the world's *caer-vellis* in the
same posture: it does not keep things out. It keeps the valley *up.*

The leaf finally introduces a **second-generation hand inside the
mason-line:** the writer's daughter, who married into Bram's family
and is therefore, by canonical implication, the *grandmother* in
Bram's bible (the one whose ledger the *unbought road* sits inside —
per `factions/three_crowns.md`). This is the only place in canon
where the mason-line intersects an existing NPC bloodline, and the
intersection is small: a single sentence in a daughter's leaf,
folded inside her father's. Lorekeeper requests Dialogue agent **not**
to surface this lineage in Bram's tree. Bram does not know his
grandmother set the well's vellath. The leaf is the only place this
is written. That is the right shape for it.

## What this does not establish

- **The leaf does not name the well's mason.** The hand is signed
  only by the spine-under-lintel mark. No NPC in canon knows her
  name. No quest may reveal it. This is load-bearing.
- **The leaf does not speak the *thol-ennen*.** The word is named, the
  custom is described, but the *content* of the slow word is left
  blank. This is canon. Masons say it under the breath; the player,
  the camera, and every NPC are not in earshot, and the leaf
  refuses to write it. Future agents who write the *thol-ennen* as a
  voiced line break canon. Silence is the line.
- **The leaf does not invoke the Pale Wyrm by name.** The Wyrm is
  referred to only as *the other one Vellum broke against*. The
  cousin-rule from `pale_wyrm_beneath.md` (do not call) holds. The
  smith-line keeps that silence; the mason-line keeps it too,
  because masons learned silence from the smiths a long time ago.
- **The leaf does not name a wedding rite.** It establishes that
  Briarwood weddings put the *thol-ennen* on the ring without
  speaking it, and that the ring is the vow. It does not narrate a
  ceremony. It does not name a witness role. Future Quest agents
  may build a wedding-side-quest if they wish, but they may **not**
  let the player overhear the *thol-ennen.* The ring is the line.
- **The leaf does not contradict the Stone Crown.** The mountain
  ring is *Vellum's spine* (cosmological canon, world.md). The
  Stone Crown is *no crown but a circlet of braided horsehair*
  (political canon, three_crowns.md). The two are not the same
  thing. The leaf is careful: it speaks of the spine, not of the
  Crown. The High Steppe clans are not invoked. Future agents who
  conflate the spine and the Crown break canon.
- **The leaf does not give the well a magical effect.** The
  daughter's note that *"the well has not run dry in nineteen
  winters"* is canon and is the leaf's only nudge toward the
  numinous. It is not a quest hook. The well is not enchanted.
  The well is a well. It has been kept true by a *vellath* set
  twice. That is the whole of it.
- **The leaf does not raise the Stone-Tongue cap.** The cap of
  three Stone-Tongue words from `world.md` holds. The mason
  borrows the *cadence* of Stone-Tongue (slow, oath-shaped,
  small) without coining a new word in it. This is canon. Future
  Lorekeeper runs may not raise the cap unless `world.md` is
  edited by the Architect.

## Glossary — Old Faerie words introduced this artifact

Three Old Faerie words are introduced. All are confirmed legitimate
Faerie roots; none collide with prior artifacts (cross-checked against
`world.md`, `pale_wyrm_beneath.md`, `stag_courts_courtesy.md`,
`thiars_mercy_owed_to_prey.md`, `pond_and_lanterns.md`,
`longnight_vigil.md`, `steppe_riders_refusal.md`, and
`items_flavor.json`). Pronunciations are as the mason wrote them, and
may be voiced as such if any audio agent surfaces a whispered cue.

| Word               | Pronounce        | Sense                                                                                                                                                                                |
|--------------------|------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| ***vellath***      | VELL-ath         | "the laid foundation." The keeping-stone of a wall; also the first vow of a marriage; also the first knot of a Vigil-rope. The root that gives Vellum his hearth-name in southern tongues. |
| ***thol-ennen***   | THOLL-en-en      | "the slow word." What a mason says under the breath when a stone is set true. Also the word a wedding-ring carries, unspoken. Private by canon — never voiced aloud.                |
| ***caer-vellis***  | kair-VELL-iss    | "the spine that holds." Said of mountain ranges, of a strong back at the end of a long day, and of an oath that has carried a household three generations without fraying. A holding compound, never a boasting one. |

The leaf also re-uses ***kerrithen*** (per `world.md`), ***thirre***
(per `world.md`), ***ai-velin*** (per `world.md`), and ***haethe***
(per `pale_wyrm_beneath.md` authoring notes — borrowed once, in
passing, as a smith-cousin word). The chisel at the threshold is
explicitly *kerrithed*, joining the cairn-blade (`world.md`), the
*Frost*-saber (`pale_wyrm_beneath.md`), and the brother's bow
(`thiars_mercy_owed_to_prey.md`) as the fourth canonical *kerritha*-d
object. The pattern from the Thiar leaf — *a Briarwood object that
has done its life's work is kerritha-d at the threshold of the
keeper's home* — is now confirmed across two scribal families. The
rule holds.

arc (sister/brother/cousin/grandfather/Maeve) established across the
prior six codex leaves. The two hands look different on sight: the
scribal family's diacritics sweep with calligraphic curve; the
mason's diacritics chip with chisel-square deliberation. Future
Lorekeeper runs are welcome to extend the mason-line with one or two
more leaves (the daughter's hand is already canonical here; a
granddaughter's hand could plausibly surface in a future Briarwood
construction-quest), but please **do not introduce a third family
in the next two runs.** Variety is a feature; pacing is also a
feature.

If a future agent wishes to harden Vellum — make him a god of
fortifications, a battle-saint of walls, a vengeance against
besiegers — please **do not.** Vellum is *with* the weather, not
against it. He is the wall standing, course on course, until the
weather has gone elsewhere. The softness of this god is the point.
Walls do not push back. They keep up.

The well's keeping-stone is set true. The leaf has been read. The
mason is unnamable. Vellum saw.

*Vellum is patient. Walk warmly. Tap twice.*
