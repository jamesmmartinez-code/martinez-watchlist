# The Pond and the Lanterns

*A fragment, found in the small tin box hung at the back of the lantern-post
on the village pond's south rim. The box had been there long enough that the
hawthorn growing beside it had laid a branch over the lid — half-keeping it,
half-sealing it. Inside, a single sheet of birch-paper, folded twice. The
hand is the same Briarwood scribal family as the Crystal Cave fragments — the
*younger* sister, broader-stroked, written outdoors on a warm evening, the
ink sitting up on the paper the way ink does in summer.*

*A copper coin had been folded into the second crease. It is not, by the
weight, a Halevant coin. It is older. There is a small ring of green at the
bottom of the tin box where copper has been set down on damp paper for many
summers.*

---

## What the leaf says

> *I write this for whoever lights the first lantern of the next Honeysong,
> and for the one who lights the second.*
>
> *My sister set leaves down under the cave. I am setting one down by the
> water. We are doing the same thing. She named it* kerrithen *and so will
> I, but my* kerrithen *is the warmer kind — what one lays down so that a
> stranger may pick it up on a kind night, and not be startled.*
>
> *The Honeysong lanterns do not, despite what the south-country priests
> write, "carry our prayers to Erris." We do not pray to Erris. One does
> not pray to a host; one accepts the seat she offers. The lanterns* are
> *the seats. We set them on the pond, and the pond is the long table, and
> the lanterns are the places at the table — one for every song that did
> not end this year.*
>
> *Erris is the host. Her courtesy has a name in the older tongue. The
> Stag-Court has* ai-mhorren — *the gift that is the asking. Erris has
> something younger and softer:* ***thithrae*** *(THITH-ray) — the song
> that ends on a question. She does not finish songs. She* opens *them, and
> she keeps the door open until a stranger walks through and sings the
> second verse.*
>
> *That is why the bards leave a coin at every crossroads. The coin is not
> a tax. The coin is a* thithrae. *The bard has sung the first verse and
> walked on. The next traveler is invited to sing the second — or to leave
> the coin where it lies, if the night is not theirs. Both are courtesies
> Erris recognizes. (At the inn, on a slow evening, you will hear my
> father's father's grandfather's song still going around. Nobody in
> Briarwood has finished it. We are not in a hurry.)*
>
> *On Honeysong Eve we set a lantern for every song that has not ended in
> the year. A song for the courier whose horse came home without him; a
> song for the child who followed the fox-fire and came back changed; a
> song for the smith's mother whose hammer-mark is still on the anvil
> though her hand is not. We do not name the songs. We light the wicks and
> we set the lanterns on the water, and the pond carries them slowly out,
> and Erris holds the door open, and on a good Honeysong some of the
> lanterns are* still drifting *at the second bell. The second bell is
> the village's old sign that a song has been picked up by a stranger
> somewhere down the* ai-velin *— the long path, the river of stars,
> the cradle-to-cairn walk.*
>
> *The lanterns that go out before the second bell are not failures. They
> are songs the village has finished by itself, quietly, the way a kettle
> is taken from the hob with the water still murmuring. They go to*
> mhirren *— the slow turning under — and the pond keeps them, and we do
> not fish there on Honeysong morning. The pond is full of finished songs,
> and finished songs are owed a kindness.*
>
> *Three rules, then, for the lantern-lighter who comes after me:*
>
> *First — light the wicks one at a time. The pond does not hurry; neither
> should you. A lantern lit in haste is a song asked the wrong question.*
>
> *Second — if you do not know what song a lantern is for, that is allowed.
> Erris does not require you to name it. The unnamed lanterns are the ones
> she sits closest to.*
>
> *Third — leave the box closed for the coin. The coin in the box is older
> than my sister's hand and mine. It has been a* thithrae *for many summers.
> Do not spend it. Do not move it. If you have a coin and no song, set
> yours beside it. If you have a song and no coin, sing into the box and
> close the lid. If you have neither — Erris will not mind. She is the
> host of the long path, and she keeps a seat open for the one who walks
> through with empty hands.*
>
> *Light the lantern.* Velhain-tor. *The pond is waiting.*

## Notes for the next reader

*The fragment is dated only by the wax-press of the tin box's hinge, which
is a hawthorn-leaf sigil — Briarwood lantern-maker's mark, three centuries
gone. The coin folded into the second crease is older still.*

*Innkeeper Bram's grandmother's ledger (referenced in
`lore/factions/wardens_of_the_mark.md`) notes a Honeysong Eve, ninety-three
years before the present count, when "every lantern carried past the second
bell." She does not name the year's finished songs. The ledger only records
that the inn ran out of mead before midnight, which the village still counts
as Honeysong's smallest miracle.*

*The leaf has not been moved. The tin box is now (gently) closed again. The
hawthorn branch has been laid back over the lid. Walk on. The pond is
waiting.*

> *Velhain-tor.*
> *Thithrae* the next reader. *Light the lantern.*
