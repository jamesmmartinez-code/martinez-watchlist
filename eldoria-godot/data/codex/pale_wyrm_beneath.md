# The Pale Wyrm Beneath

*A fragment, found in the Crystal Caves on the down-slope side of the
under-stream where the wall sweats cold. A single sheet of birch-paper,
pressed flat against the stone by a flat stone laid over it — not hidden,
exactly, but* kerritha *—* set down, *the way a good knife is set down on
a cutting board so the next cook may find it edge-out, ready, and not be
startled. The hand is the same Briarwood scribe's that wrote the leaf upstream:
careful, sloping, the older Faerie diacritics drawn slowly, as though their
shapes were the message and the words a kindness around the shapes.*

---

## What the leaf says

> *I write this for the smith who comes after me, and for her apprentice,
> and for the apprentice's apprentice. If you are not of a forge, please
> put this back where you found it, and walk gently. The cave can hear
> you, and it does not mind, but the* thirre *under your boots is
> listening at half a depth.*
>
> *On the second evening of Wolfwake I came down to the under-stream
> because the steel I had cooled that morning had hummed when I struck
> it. Hummed, then sang, low — the way a kettle sings when it is just
> off the hob. I knew the song. So did Brigid. She does not make that
> song.*
>
> *I came down with the blade and a candle and an apology. I sat on the
> stone shelf above the underflow, and I laid the blade down — point to
> the dark — and I said the word my grandmother taught me, that her
> grandmother had taught her, and that no one south of the Mountain
> Ring will say aloud:* ***mhirren.*** *The slow turning under.*
>
> *That is what is beneath the cave. Not the Wyrm itself. The Wyrm's*
> ***mhirren.*** *Its sleep that is not sleep. The dreaming of a thing
> that is not gone, but only set down — the way Vellum is set down, and
> the way the long roads are set down between two travelings.*
>
> *I have read the south-country priests on this. They write that the
> Pale Wyrm is dead. They are wrong, but they are not unkind to be
> wrong; the wrongness keeps the smoke-cities steady, and a steady
> smoke-city is not a small thing. Let them have it. We who hammer
> iron in the valley have to know better, because the iron will tell
> us.*
>
> *Here is what the iron tells us, set down plainly so the next forge
> may not have to learn it twice:*
>
> *— A blade that is forged on the third night of Wolfwake, near the
> mountain side of the village, will sometimes carry a thread of frost
> in its temper. Not always. Not most of the time. But sometimes. The
> frost is not a flaw. The frost is the Wyrm's* mhirren *passing through
> the steel the way a draft passes through a door that has not quite
> been closed.*
>
> *— A blade with that thread in it will keep a sharper edge than a
> blade without. It will also, on cold mornings, sing the kettle-song
> when it is unsheathed. It will also, in the hand of someone who has
> not yet learned to be patient with iron, run a little eager — pull
> a little forward in the swing — as though it were trying to wake
> the thing it remembers.*
>
> *— You may forge such a blade. You may sell such a blade. You may
> gift such a blade. You must not name such a blade* Frost. *Not in
> the village square. Not on a banner. Not on the tang.*
>
> *The naming is the door-shape. To name a thing is to call it. The
> Court taught us this in the older language and we have been slow
> students. The Wyrm is patient now. Wyrms are. But the Wyrm has not
> agreed to be called, and a* mhirren *is undone by a calling.*
>
> *I have asked Brigid about this twice. Both times she answered the
> same way, which is the way she answers when the answer is already
> written into the work: she let the coals settle, and she did not
> name the song the steel was singing. That is the answer. That is
> the whole answer.*
>
> *If you forge a Frost-thread blade — and you will, if you forge long
> enough — call it by the wielder. Call it by the river it is meant
> to cross. Call it by the grandmother whose stew it will defend. Call
> it by anything you like, except the one word.*
>
> *And when the wielder dies, and the blade comes back to the forge —
> and it does come back; that is what good steel does — set it down
> here. Not in the forge-fire. Not in the hearth. Here, on the under-
> stream rock, with a stone over the tang. The cave is patient. The
> cave will keep the asking.*
>
> ***Velhain-tor*** *to whoever reads this. Walk warmly. Walk slowly.
> If the kettle on your forge sings without water in it, do not be
> startled — only listen, and bow once, and go on with the work.*
>
> — *signed only with a forge-mark: an open hand above an anvil. The
> ink has feathered along the edge of the stone, where the wall sweats.*

## What the village does, having found this

(Recorded by the next scribe down. The hand is younger; the diacritics are
straighter; the spelling of *mhirren* drifts a little, settles, drifts again.)

> *The leaf was found in Honeysong of the year the bridge was rebuilt.
> Maeve read it twice and said* "good," *which is what Maeve says when
> a thing was already known but is now also written. The leaf was
> read aloud once at Brigid's anvil — Edda's great-aunt, then —
> and the Forge-Mother's mark on the anvil-face flickered once,
> like a candle when a door has been shut nearby.* Mhirren *passed
> into the smith-tongue that night. It does not appear in the
> south-country dictionaries. Lyra's grandmother knew it from her
> grandmother and was glad to find it set down.*
>
> *We have not, so far as the village remembers, named a blade* Frost.
> *We have named a kettle so, once, in jest, and unnamed it the same
> evening when the kettle began to sing without water in it. The
> kitchen in question is no longer in use; the stove is fine. The
> kettle was* kerritha *to the under-stream rock, with a stone laid
> over its handle. The cave keeps it.*
>
> *Smiths' apprentices: read this leaf before your first* haethe.
> *Read it again before your first commissioned blade. Read it a
> third time before any work you do on the third night of Wolfwake.
> If the steel sings without your striking it, set down the hammer,
> set down the work, sit on the shelf above the under-stream, and
> say* mhirren *once, softly. Then go back up the path. The work
> will be there in the morning. The work is patient. The Wyrm is
> patient. We are the only ones in this valley who hurry.*
>
> *Tend the Vigil candles. Tend Erris's lanterns. Tend the herb beds.
> Do not name a blade* Frost.
>
> — *the second hand stops here. There is a pressed sprig of marshmint
> tucked into the fold; it has not lost its color, even after all these
> years, which is a small mercy and a small thirre, and we will not
> ask the herb why.*

## Three further notes (this scribe, hand untrained but earnest)

1. **The cave-singer.** A Frost-thread blade may, on a cold morning, sing
   the kettle-song when drawn. This is not a curse. It is a courtesy. The
   blade is asking permission of the *thirre* beneath it. Sheath it
   gently for a count of three, then unsheath it again. The song will not
   return until the next cold morning, by which time the wielder is meant
   to have remembered to be patient.

2. **The forge-mother's silence.** Brigid the Forge-Mother does not
   speak the Wyrm's name. She is older than the Sundering by a margin
   none of us can measure, and she has chosen, over those long quiet
   years, what she will and will not say. A smith who keeps that silence
   is keeping a promise. A smith who breaks it is not punished — only
   politely declined the next blade's *haethe*.

3. **The wielder's part.** If you carry a Frost-thread blade and find
   yourself growing eager in the swing — pulled a half-inch forward, as
   though the blade were leaning toward something — stop. Set the blade
   down on stone (any stone; the cave is kind). Breathe three times. The
   blade is remembering its *mhirren* and asking you to remember yours.
   Most of the time, what the blade is leaning toward is *not* the thing
   you should be cutting. The blade does not know this yet. You do.
