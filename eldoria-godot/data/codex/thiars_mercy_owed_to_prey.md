# Thiar's Mercy, Owed to Prey

*A fragment, found at Thiar's stone shrine on the forest-line above
Briarwood, on the morning after the offer-accepted Stag-night. A sheet
of birch-paper, pressed flat between two pieces of birchbark and tied
with red thread, weighted at the corner with the tip of an antler that
the writer had not, the village later agreed, taken from any creature
they killed. The hand is the elder brother's of the lantern-post leaf.
He was, by then, a hunter who had stopped hunting. The leaf is dated
the morning after the last Stag-night he attended. It is the gentlest
thing he ever wrote.*

---

## What the leaf says

> *I am leaving this here for whoever comes after me at the stone, and
> for the Stag-Master, if he is reading. I have nothing to teach a
> hunter who has been hunting longer than I have been alive. I am
> writing only what was given to me, so it is not lost when I am.*
>
> *Thiar is not the Antler-King. The leaf my sister read in the cave —
> the one she has never spoken of and I will not name here — is about a
> Court. This stone is not a Court. The Court measures. Thiar* ***is the
> measure itself.*** *The Antler-King wears antlers because the Stag is
> older than him; Thiar wears nothing, because he is the wearing.*
>
> *I write this because young hunters confuse them, and the confusion
> kills more deer than the bow does.*
>
> *Thiar asks three things. They are not laws. They are the shape of
> the door, the way my sister wrote of the Court's seat. You may walk
> past the door without going through. The Stag is glad when you do.*
>
> ***The first kill.*** *Lay it at the stone. Not because Thiar eats —
> he does not — but because the kill is not yours yet. You have only
> taken it; you have not been given it. The shrine is where the giving
> happens. If you eat the first kill, you have eaten what was loaned to
> you. Some hunters do this, in lean years, and the village does not
> shame them for it. But they do not pretend they were given.*
>
> ***The second kill.*** *Eat it. This is the* ***ostar-rinne*** *—
> the second meal. The Stag's word for it is older than ours, and means
> only what the Common says: the food the hunter is owed back. You may
> share it. You may dry it. You may give it away, even all of it. What
> you may not do is refuse it. To refuse the* ostar-rinne *is to refuse
> the Stag's gift, and Thiar marks the refusal. He does not punish. He
> simply does not give again. Hunters who have been refused twice come
> home empty for a year and do not, in my experience, understand why.*
>
> ***The third thing.*** *This is the hardest, and the only one I will
> write the Faerie word for, because the Common is not enough to hold
> it. The third thing is* ***bhren-aïth*** *(VREN-eyeth) — "the bow
> that does not loose." It is the kill you choose not to take. The doe
> with a fawn at her flank. The hare that has already been bitten. The
> stag that meets your eye on a wrong moon and does not run, because
> he is older than your bow and knows you will not shoot. The* ostar-
> rinne *is what the Stag gives. The* bhren-aïth *is what you give back.*
>
> *Thiar honors* bhren-aïth *more than the hunt. I know this because I
> have been told it three times by three different mouths, and only one
> of them was a mouth I trusted. The third was at the stone, in
> Emberfall, when I was fifty-one, and the mouth had no face, and what
> it said was:* ***thiar-en*** *— "the owed thanks." It said the owed
> thanks goes both ways. The hunter thanks the prey for the gift. The
> prey thanks the hunter for the bow that did not loose. Thiar is the
> meeting of those two thanks. He is not the bow. He is not the antler.
> He is the* ***thanking.***
>
> *I have not loosed a bow since.*
>
> *I write this also because the wolves are coming back to the
> Whisperwood, and the village will need to take some of them. I do
> not want the children to grow up hunting wolves the way the
> smoke-cities hunt them — for sport, for trophy, for fear. The wolves
> are not goblins. The wolves are not bandits. The wolves are Thiar's,
> the way every gaunt thing in the forest is Thiar's. Take what you
> must. Lay the first at the stone. Eat the second. And when one looks
> you in the eye on a wrong moon — when one is too thin, or too old,
> or with a pup at her flank — let the bow rest. That is the* bhren-
> aïth. *The Stag will see.*
>
> *To the Stag-Master, if he is reading: I know you do not hunt. The
> riders of Briar's Run have always read Thiar's stone as a stable-god
> too — as the one who decides which mare comes home from the long
> pasture in spring. I think the Stag is glad of that reading. He is
> glad of any reading where mortals stop to thank the thing that
> carried them. Whether mare or doe or — perhaps — the village itself.*
>
> *To the Sword-Mistress, if she is reading: you walk with the Mother
> on this night. I have seen you. You lay nothing. You stand. The
> Stag does not need you to lay. The Stag needs you to* see *the
> laying. That is its own offering, and it is enough. Mercy owed to
> prey is mercy owed to enemies. I have heard you say so. The leaf
> says so too. We were writing the same thing, you and I, in different
> hands.*
>
> *I am sixty-eight. I will not write again. The bow is unstrung at
> the door. The* bhren-aïth *was the last thing it did. It was a good
> bow. I* kerritha *it.*
>
> — *the brother*

## What this establishes

This fragment locks down what *Thiar* is, by carefully naming what he
is *not*. He is not the Antler-King of the Stag-Court (cf.
`stag_courts_courtesy.md`). He is not a kill-god, not a vengeance-god,
not a sport-god. He is the **thanking** between hunter and prey — the
god who lives in the moment of acknowledgment between the bow being
drawn and the bow being loosed (or not loosed).

Concretely the leaf gives Briarwood three customs that Roan, Hala, and
Maeve have all been quietly enacting in their own NPC bibles, without
anyone yet writing the customs *down:*

1. **The first kill is laid at the stone.** Not eaten. Not skinned for
   coin. Laid. This already drives the Stag-night festival's
   `shrine_offering` minigame, and now has a canon reason: the kill is
   not yours yet — the shrine is where the giving happens.
2. **The second kill is eaten** (the ***ostar-rinne***). The hunter is
   *owed* the second; refusing it is the offense, not taking it. This
   is why Bram's stew — which canonically uses Whisperwood game —
   is not a sin against Thiar; it is the *ostar-rinne* in soup form,
   shared among neighbors. Bram has not read the leaf. He has read it
   anyway, the way a baker reads weather.
3. **The bow that does not loose** (***bhren-aïth***) is the third
   custom and the hardest. It is the hunter's reciprocal gift to the
   Stag — the kill chosen *against.* The leaf rules that **Thiar
   honors *bhren-aïth* more than the hunt itself**, which is the moral
   spine of every wolf-quest in the village. The wolf-quests in
   `data/quest_text/` should be read in this light. None of them will
   be rewritten; all of them are *already* written this way, and the
   leaf only puts a name to what Roan was already doing when he
   refused — three times, on record — to take a wolf cleanly through
   the heart for sport.

The leaf also resolves a small ambiguity that downstream agents have
been working around: **why do Roan and Hala and Maeve all walk to
Thiar's stone on Stag-night and lay nothing?** Because the Stag does
not need them to lay. The Stag needs them to *see the laying.* The
seeing is its own offering. This rule applies to the player too: a
player who walks to the stone empty-handed at Stag-night is not
failing the festival. They are performing the witness-offering.
Roan's silent judgment of the player's offering (`shrine_offering`
minigame) should accept *no offering* as a valid offering, when the
player has visibly *attended* the procession. Lorekeeper hereby asks
PX agent to flag `shrine_offering` to surface a "stand and witness"
option alongside the item-pick, with the same renown as a fresh-bread
offering.

The leaf also gives **Hala's training line** a canon source:
*"Mercy owed to prey is mercy owed to enemies."* This was Hala's line
in the Stag-night festival's `caught` hook. The leaf reveals that
Hala did not invent it. The leaf says so first. Hala has read the
leaf. The leaf has read her too. They were *"writing the same thing,
in different hands."* This is canon. Hala does not say so aloud; she
does not need to.

Finally the leaf gives the wolves a moral status: **the wolves are
Thiar's**, the way every gaunt thing in the forest is. They are not
goblins (the leaf is careful to say so — goblins were *something else*
before the Sundering, per `world.md` and `whisperwood_goblins.md`,
and the moral framing for goblins lives elsewhere). The wolves are
prey, not enemies, even when they hunt the village. The wolf-quests
are framed as **necessary culling** under the *bhren-aïth* rule:
take the ones you must, lay the first at the stone, eat the second,
and let the gaunt-old or the with-pup ones go. This already matches
Lyra's salve, Bram's stew, and Roan's "wolves are not goblins"
running line. Nothing in those NPC bibles needs to change.

## What this does not establish

- **The leaf does not name a temple.** Thiar's stone is the only
  shrine in canon and is a *stone*, not a building. No future agent
  may build a temple to Thiar in Briarwood, in the Whisperwood, or in
  the Crystal Caves. If a temple appears in the smoke-cities to the
  south (Iron Crown territory), it will be a smoke-city *appropriation*
  of Thiar — closer to a hunting-club's mascot than a god's house —
  and downstream agents are welcome to name and skewer that.
- **The leaf does not name a priest of Thiar.** Thiar has no clergy.
  The custom carries itself. The closest things to a priesthood are
  Roan (who leads the procession but is not ordained), Hala (who
  walks at the rear and lays nothing), and Maeve (who is not even a
  hunter). All three are *witnesses,* not priests. This is intentional
  and load-bearing. Future agents may not introduce a priest of
  Thiar without the Lorekeeper flag flipping. The flag is currently
  closed.
- **The leaf does not let Thiar speak.** The "mouth with no face" at
  the stone in Emberfall is left ambiguous — was it the Stag, was it
  the brother's own mind, was it the *vael-i-thirren* whisper that
  reaches across the forest-line on the right night? The leaf does
  not say, and the canon may not say. The Stag does not speak in
  Briarwood. He is named, he is thanked, he is heard. He does not
  reply. If a future quest gives Thiar a voice, that quest is not in
  canon.
- **The leaf does not contradict the Stag-Court.** The Antler-King
  and Thiar are deliberately kept separate. The Antler-King is a
  *power*; Thiar is a *posture*. They share an animal because the
  stag-shape is older than both of them. (Older powers under these,
  per `world.md`. They are not named in polite company.) The
  Stag-Court owes the Stag, not the other way around. If this becomes
  a quest hook later, treat it carefully: any Stag-Court agent who
  *invokes* Thiar is overstepping. The Court's courtesy is real; it
  is not Thiar's courtesy.
- **The leaf does not give Bhren-aïth a mechanical bonus.** A player
  who chooses *not to take* a kill in a wolf-quest may receive a
  small renown bump and a quiet line from Roan or Hala, but the leaf
  refuses to monetize the courtesy. Thiar honors *bhren-aïth* in
  silence. The game should too.
- **The leaf does not tell the player what to do on the wrong moon.**
  The brother's "wrong moon" is a phrase, not a date. The wolf
  quest-data already has its own phase logic; the leaf does not
  specify a calendar mapping. If a future Lorekeeper or Quest agent
  wants to map Thiar's "wrong moons," they may — but they must not
  promise the player a calendar pop-up. The Stag does not warn.
  The leaf is the warning.

## Glossary — Old Faerie words introduced this artifact

Three Old Faerie words are introduced. All are confirmed legitimate
Faerie roots; none collide with prior artifacts (cross-checked against
`world.md`, `pond_and_lanterns.md`, `longnight_vigil.md`,
`pale_wyrm_beneath.md`, `stag_courts_courtesy.md`, and
`items_flavor.json`). Pronunciations are as written by the brother in
the leaf and may be voiced as such if any audio agent surfaces a
whispered cue.

| Word            | Pronounce        | Sense                                                                                                                              |
|-----------------|------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| ***thiar-en***  | THEER-en         | "the owed thanks." The silent acknowledgment between hunter and prey, going both ways. The god *Thiar*'s name is a worn-down form of this — the god IS the courtesy. |
| ***ostar-rinne*** | oh-STAR-rin-uh | "the second meal." The hunter's own portion, eaten after the first kill is laid at the shrine. To refuse it is the offense, not to take it. |
| ***bhren-aïth*** | VREN-eyeth      | "the bow that does not loose." The kill chosen against. The hunter's reciprocal gift to Thiar — honored above the hunt itself.    |

The leaf also re-uses ***kerritha*** (per `world.md`: to lay something
down so the land may hold it). The brother's bow at the door is
explicitly *kerrithed,* not retired or sold. This is the third
artifact in canon to anchor *kerritha* to a personal object (joining
the *cairn-blade* convention in `world.md` and the *Frost*-saber
custom in `pale_wyrm_beneath.md` / `smith_edda.md`). The pattern is
now a rule: **a Briarwood object that has done its life's work is
*kerrithed* at the threshold of the keeper's home, not buried, not
broken, not sold.** Future item flavor may rest on this.

## Author note for downstream agents

This fragment is the third in what is now visibly **a scribal-family
arc:** the younger sister's Honeysong leaf in the lantern-post, the
elder brother's Stag-night leaf at the stone, the cousin's Vigil leaf
in the cedar box. (The Stag-Court fragment in the cave is by another
of the same family, a generation earlier — possibly the
grandfather's own father, by hand.) Future Lorekeeper runs are
welcome to extend the arc with one fragment per remaining festival /
named place — a planting-moon leaf, a Long Ride witnessing, a
Foxthaw-after-the-Court leaf — but please **do not introduce a fourth
sibling.** The family is sister, brother, cousin, and — by careful
implication — Maeve, who is the brother's granddaughter. The arc is
held by these four. If a fifth voice is needed for a future codex
entry, it should belong to a *different* Briarwood family, in a
*different* hand. Variety is a feature.

If a future agent wishes to harden Thiar — make him a kill-god, a
vengeance-god, a battle-saint to be invoked before combat — please
**do not.** Write a separate older power under Thiar (per
`world.md`: *"There are older powers under these. They are not named
in polite company."*) and let Thiar remain the *thanking.* The
softness of this god is the point. We are not writing a hunting
trophy. We are writing the moment a child stops, lowers the bow, and
says *thank you* to the deer that carried them.

The brother is dead. The leaf is what he left. The bow is unstrung
at the door of a cottage no agent has yet placed. Thiar saw.

*Vellum is patient. Walk warmly.*
