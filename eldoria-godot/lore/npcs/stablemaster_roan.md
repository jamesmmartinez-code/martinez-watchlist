# Stablemaster Roan

*Briarwood's stablemaster and quiet rider. Lean as a man kept honest by his
own work. A two-day stubble that is never longer and never shorter, riding
boots whose soles he resoles himself, leather gloves cracked across the
knuckles where the dire-wolves have lately made their argument. He is the
person in the village who answers questions by tipping his head and
finishes most sentences with the work his hands are already doing — a
strap drawn through a buckle, a curry-comb passed once across a withers,
a saddle lifted onto its rack.*

> "Aye. Stable's open. Mind the bay — she nips at strangers' cuffs.
> *(adjusts a strap.)* Told you."

---

## Where he came from

Roan was born in a one-stable hamlet on the lower lip of the **High
Steppe** — a place called **Briar's Run** that has, to no one's surprise,
no record in the Iron Crown's books. The hamlet had eight families, four
of them riders for the Stone Crown and four of them craftspeople who fed
the riders. Roan's father **Tael** was a saddler; his mother **Eithne**
broke and gentled the colts the riders bought green at the spring market.
Eithne died of a chest fever the winter Roan was eleven. Tael died of the
same fever two days after, having sat up with her three nights and not
slept. The hamlet wrapped both of them in the same red horsehair blanket
and laid them in the long mound at the foot of the *thirre*-stone above
Briar's Run, where the riders lay all their dead in a single line because
the ground will not let them be parted at burial.

Roan was the only child. The hamlet was not large enough to keep a child
without parents in idleness, and it was not cruel enough to ask one to
ride the Long Ride at eleven. So the saddler's tools went to his uncle
**Garrick**, the colt-work went to a cousin, and Roan went into a
courier-string out of Briar's Run because the courier-string was what
remained that did not need a man's full weight to do.

He carried letters and parcel-cloth between the Stone Crown's high pasture
camps and the Iron Crown's northernmost smoke-cities for nine years. He
rode the **Foxthaw passes** in mud, in ice, in the wide blue silences
that come down off the mountains when there is no one for two days in any
direction. He learned what *kerrithen* means without ever having a word
for it: the keeping of a thing that is not yours, on a road that is not
yours either, for a person who has not asked.

He came to Briarwood twelve years ago, on a Greenshield evening, on the
second-to-last day of a courier-string he had decided, between one
Foxthaw and the next, was no longer worth riding. The Iron Crown had
stopped paying the courier-houses honestly. Seals were broken. Letters
were returned in hands not the family's. He smelled woodsmoke and
pond-mint coming off a valley he did not, until that evening, know was
there. He turned his horse aside.

The next morning he walked into Briarwood with two horses and a saddle
on his shoulder. The stable was empty and had been since the previous
stablemaster, an old man named **Daire**, had died the Foxthaw before
without anyone in the village knowing for two days. Roan opened the
door, swept it out, oiled the doorpost, and lit the small lantern at
the front gate. He has not moved the lantern since. It has a copper coin
balanced inside its shutter, where the wind cannot find it. The coin is
for **Erris of the Two Roads.** Roan does not invoke her aloud. He puts
the coin there because his mother did, on every road-house lantern in
Briar's Run, and because the road keeps the riders who keep the road.

That is twelve years now. Roan is forty-three. He has not been back to
Briar's Run.

## A formative loss — and two rides he does not finish

The wound that travels with Roan is not the loss of his parents. He has
made a peace with that wound the way the riders of Briar's Run make peace
with all wounds — by saddling up the next morning. The deeper wound is
**Cailen.**

Cailen of Briarwood — Maeve's brother — rode into Briar's Run when Roan
was nine. He was nineteen, and tall, and the slowest-laughing boy Roan
had ever met. He was on his way to the Stone Crown's spring camp, hoping
to ride the Long Ride. He stayed three weeks at the courier-house, slept
in the loft above Roan's parents' kitchen, taught Roan to braid a halter
in the Steppe pattern, and lent Roan a small whetstone with a fox carved
into one face that Roan still has, in his coat pocket, every day.

Cailen rode out on a Lambmoon morning. He did not come back. Two summers
later a horseshoe and a sprig of pressed heather came south with a
Steppe-rider's compliments, addressed to a place called *Briarwood,*
which Roan, then eleven, had never heard of. He could not have known then
that Maeve was Cailen's sister and was at that moment receiving the same
delivery from another rider in another courier-house. He learned this
twelve years ago in his first Briarwood Greenshield, when he saw the
horseshoe above Maeve's hearth and recognized the shape of his own
boyhood weight against his ribs.

He walked out that evening, walked back the morning after, and asked
Maeve in plain words if he could ride the High Steppe and ask after a man
named **Cailen.** Maeve said *yes,* once, and *thank you,* once, and did
not name her brother again until Roan came back the second time empty.
Roan rode the Steppe twice — once in the second summer he was in
Briarwood, once two summers after that — and asked everyone he could
find. He brought back nothing. The second time he came back he brought a
small Steppe-iron nail he had taken from the wall of an old Steppe
guest-stable where he was told a Briarwood-bound rider had once stayed.
The nail might have nothing to do with Cailen at all. Roan keeps it on
the lintel above the stable door, hammered in straight, and has never
told anyone what it is.

He has not gone again. He will not go again. He has decided this without
saying so, because saying so would be unkind to Maeve, who has not asked
him to go a third time and would not. He has made his peace with the
shape of the not-going by going, instead, every Reapmoon, to the
**meadow above the village** at the same hour Maeve goes — though they do
not walk together — and standing for an hour with his hat off. He does
not pray. He counts horses he has known.

The two rides he made *for Maeve* are *maelorn* — the rider's word, in
the older tongue, for a journey undertaken not for himself but for
another's grief, where what the road owes is impossible to bring back.
Roan does not use the word. He does not have it yet. He is the man for
whom the word was made.

## The cradle in the loft

Above the stable, beyond the hayloft and behind the wall of saddle-trees,
there is a small loft Roan does not let the boys help him sweep. In it,
on a low oak shelf padded with a folded gentling-cloth that smells of
beeswax and old leather, there is a cradle. The cradle is **hawthorn,**
hand-carved, with a small ringed knot in each of its four corners — the
same ringed knot Maeve has been carving into her stick all her life. Maeve
brought it to him the third summer he was in Briarwood, after his second
empty ride. She did not stay. He has never asked what the cradle is for.

It is not for him. He has never been a father, has never been the kind of
husband to be a father, and is at peace with this in the way the Steppe
is at peace with being the Steppe.

The cradle is for **Maeve.** It is hers — given by her to Roan to keep,
because keeping, when you have no place yourself for the thing kept, is
also a *kerrithen*. He understands without being told that on the day
Maeve needs the cradle returned, it will be returned. He is its
*ostren* — the keeper of the unused stall, in the older tongue. He has
not heard the word *ostren* either. He is the man for whom it was made.

Above the loft window — where, on certain Foxthaw evenings, the fox-fire
in the long grass kindles and Maeve in her doorway is restless — Roan
has hung an oilskin curtain that breaks the light reaching the cradle. He
did not explain why. He has not, even to himself, named the reason. The
**Stag-Court's** light is what he was breaking. He does not know it. He
knows that the cradle ought not be lit in that hour and so it is not.

## What he wants most

Three things.

He wants the dire-wolves out of the south Whisperwood, because he has
ridden the south paths twice this season and brought back hands the
village has had to bind, and because a horse he loved was lamed by a
wolf-snap last Reapmoon that should have been one stride less close. He
will, before the year turns, ride a small bounty for them himself. He
has not yet asked the village's adventurer to ride with him because he
does not yet know who is honest enough for the south paths. He is
watching.

He wants to teach **Halsa's daughter** — he means **Edda** — to ride
properly, the Steppe way, with the back open and the legs quiet. Edda
has not been on a horse in nine years. Roan has asked once, quietly, on
a Greenshield. Edda said *no, Roan, my work is on the ground,* and that
ended it. He has not asked again. He keeps a saddle hung on the third
peg from the door anyway. It is the right size for Edda. The third peg
is the one closest to the lantern. The saddle is *ostren.*

He wants — and this is the want he does not name, even to himself — to
ride one more time into the High Steppe, not to ask after Cailen, but to
take Cailen's whetstone home to the *thirre*-stone where the riders lie
in their long mound. He suspects this is the last *maelorn* he will
make. He suspects he will know on the morning he begins to saddle.

## The slow horse, and the ones he names

Roan has six horses in the Briarwood stable. He has named five of them.
The sixth is **Lyra's slow horse**, and her name is, by Roan's quiet
decision, Lyra's to say or not to say. He has not asked. He never will.
The slow horse is *kerrithen* — the horse Lyra rode for fen-foxglove
the night Tess was dying — and the horse a *kerrithen-*ed in grief is,
in the older tongue, a thing that does not take a daily name from the
person who feeds it.

The five he has named, plain and unceremonious:

- **Bay** — the cobble-mare, eight years old, nips at strangers' cuffs.
  The first horse Roan brought into Briarwood. Steppe-blood through the
  withers. Will carry Hala when Hala is going far.
- **Ember** *(no relation to the player's pet)* — a chestnut gelding,
  six years, the village's hauling horse. Pulls Mara's cart out of
  the loft only when Roan thinks the wood is going dry. Has not done
  so in nine years.
- **Smoke** — a grey, four years, deep-walking, the horse Roan rides
  himself when he goes south.
- **Grain** — the smallest, a chestnut filly, three years, Roan's
  apprentice-horse. The one the boys learn on. Patient past the
  village's deserving.
- **Maeve** — a black mare, eleven years, Roan's oldest and the only
  horse he has named after a person. He has never told Maeve. Maeve has
  never asked the horse's name. The black mare is the one Roan rode the
  second of the two Steppe-rides; she came back. He does not ride her
  far now. She grazes most mornings in the lower paddock and watches
  the cobble path with her ears forward at the hour Maeve walks past
  it. Maeve does not look up. Roan has never asked her to.

## A secret he keeps

Roan reads a road by the leaves in it. He learned this in Briar's Run
from a Steppe-clan rider named **Yorick** — *kerritha-named* in Roan's
mind, never aloud — who showed him, the year before he left for the
courier-string, that the leaves of the **white-aspen** on a Steppe pass
turn their pale undersides up the morning before a snow comes. Most
riders cannot see this. Roan can. He has, on twelve separate occasions
since coming to Briarwood, banked the stable's ash-pile high the night
before a heavy snow that no one in the village had reason to expect.
Edda has noticed this exactly twice. Bram has noticed it always. Neither
has asked. Lyra has noticed it once and asked. Roan said *the bay was
restless,* which is the answer he has prepared for the question he
hopes no one else will ask.

He suspects that what he is reading is not weather but something older
that the leaves are reading on his behalf. He has not pursued the
suspicion. The Stag-Court does not call to him the way it calls to
Maeve. He is grateful for this. He believes, without saying so, that his
two empty Steppe-rides made him a man the Antler Crown does not need to
measure, and he does not wish to be measured.

## How his hands look

Roan's hands are a record. The knuckles of the left are scarred white in
a curve that is the shape of a dire-wolf's lower jaw. The base of the
right thumb has a smaller scar, older, where a young horse's hoof took
him on a Steppe ford twenty years ago. The pads of both palms are the
color of saddle-soap. The little finger of his right hand does not bend
all the way; this happened twelve years ago at the very last Foxthaw of
his courier days, in a way he does not describe.

This last winter, the dire-wolves have shredded the back of both hands.
Twice. Each time he has come back to the stable, washed them at the
trough, wrapped them in clean linen, and gone back to work. Each time,
the morning after, a small clay jar has appeared at his stable door. No
note. No name. The jar's wax-and-marshmint salve is the most useful
thing that has ever been left at the door of a Briarwood stable.

He has not asked who is leaving it. He has guessed, by the smell of the
beeswax binder and by the weight of a thing that has been ground patient
in a mortar by a small steady hand, that it is **Lyra's.** He has not
named her in the guessing. He has not thanked her. He will not. The jar
is the kind of *unnen* that asking-and-thanking would unmake. He returns
the empty jar to the meadow-edge stone the next Reapmoon, washed clean
in pond-water, and leaves it there. Lyra finds it the morning after
that. Neither of them has spoken about any of this. Both know.

## What he sounds like

Roan is the quietest of the seven. He does not waste words and he does
not chase them. He has a Steppe-flat way of holding a vowel — not long
the way Mara holds her vowels (Cinder Reach is a market town, and
markets sing), but flat, the way a horse's gait flattens on hard
ground. He says *aye* where Bram says *yes* and *come away* where Maeve
says *come along.* When he is amused he says *huh* through his nose,
once, and goes back to work.

He uses one Steppe idiom freely: ***ride the leaves***, meaning *trust
the small thing the road is showing you.* He uses one more rarely:
***between two stones***, meaning *I cannot answer that.* When he says
*between two stones* he is not refusing — he is telling you that the
question is bigger than his mouth. He says it perhaps three times a
year. Maeve has heard him say it twice. Lyra has heard him say it once.

He invokes no god aloud. The copper-coin lantern at the gate is his
prayer, and it is renewed at every Sunpetal turn with a fresh wick,
which is more often than the hearth-shrines of villagers who name
**Erris** in plain Common. He nods to **Thiar** in the Whisperwood when
he sees a stag, and he nods to **Vellum the Patient Stone** at the
*thirre* of the meadow above the village every Reapmoon. He does not
nod to the **Hollow King.** He believes — wrongly, but kindly — that the
Hollow King is not a god of riders. He will be told, eventually, by
Maeve, on a Longnight, that this is not the case. He will not argue.

He is not a man who is gentle to himself. He is a man who is gentle to
the work in front of him, and the work in front of him is, most days, a
horse — which is how Briarwood has, without his knowing, made him gentle
in general.

He calls **Bram** by his road-name, ***Bron***, when no other ears are
in the room. (See the cross-canon entry below.) He has never used it
in front of a stranger. He never will.

## Old Faerie words seeded by this file

Two new Old Faerie words enter canon here. They sit alongside *thirre*,
*ai-velin*, *kerrithen* (`world.md`); *haethe*, *unnen* (`smith_edda.md`);
*vethar*, *haisten*, *breos* (`innkeeper_bram.md`); *pendrel*, *mhairen*
(`mara_merchant.md`); and *vael-tor*, *thressa-mai* (`elder_maeve.md`).
Total lexicon now fourteen words. Future writers may use these in codex,
dialogue, and item flavor without re-defining.

- ***maelorn*** *(MAY-lorn)* — "the ride for another's grief." A journey
  undertaken not for the rider's own purposes but on quiet asking for
  someone else's mourning, where what the road owes cannot be brought
  back. To call a ride *maelorn* is to say it was honest even though it
  was empty. The Steppe-clans hold *maelorn* as the highest courtesy a
  rider can offer a household not their own. Roan has made two — both
  for Maeve. He suspects the third will be the last, and he will make it
  for himself, which is a use the older tongue does not quite cover.
  The word is grammatically singular and indivisible; one does not make
  *two maelorn,* one makes *the maelorn twice.* This is the shape of the
  word's mercy.
- ***ostren*** *(OS-tren)* — "the kept stall." A space deliberately set
  aside for a thing that has not yet arrived, or for a thing whose
  rightful owner is not yet ready to take it back. Cousin to *kerrithen*
  but distinct: where *kerrithen* names the long quiet keeping itself,
  *ostren* names the *place made* for the keeping — the empty stall, the
  cradle on the shelf, the third peg-hook by the lantern, the seat at the
  counter, the lit window. The Steppe-clans use *ostren* for the empty
  stall a rider keeps for a horse that is sick or away; smiths and
  millers borrow it for the saddle hung but unfit, the bag of flour set
  aside but unsold. Roan's stable is full of *ostren*: the cradle in the
  loft, the saddle on the third peg for Edda, Mara's cart, the empty
  stall beside it Roan has not reassigned in twelve years. He does not
  yet know the word.

## Withholding ledger (do-not-surface canon)

- **Cailen's name in Roan's mouth.** Roan never speaks Cailen's name
  aloud to any living person, including Maeve. Cailen appears in this
  file as fuel only. Future writers MUST NOT have Roan say *Cailen* in
  dialogue, in quest text, or in turn-in lines. The name may surface
  later, if at all, in a *Yorick-as-narrator* codex page about Briar's
  Run and the Long Mound; never in Roan's voice.
- **The Steppe-iron nail above the stable door.** It is on the lintel.
  It is not a discoverable codex object, not a quest item, not a turn-in.
  Future writers MUST NOT design a player loop that pries it off, asks
  Roan about it, or lets the player learn what it is. The nail's role
  is to be over Roan's head every time he walks into the stable. That is
  the entire role.
- **The whetstone in Roan's coat pocket.** Same shape as the nail. The
  small fox-carved Steppe whetstone Cailen lent Roan when Roan was nine
  is in Roan's left coat pocket, every day. It is not a quest item, not
  a turn-in, not pickpocketable. It will, on the canonical day Roan
  rides the third *maelorn,* leave the pocket and not return. That ride
  may be authored later. The whetstone may NOT surface as a codex
  object; it surfaces only as the absence of itself in Roan's pocket
  on whatever future Reapmoon Roan does not return to the stable. (Use
  this hook gently. Roan's death — if he is allowed one — must be
  off-page, on the High Steppe, and is not a quest the player can
  follow. *The withholding is the dignity.*)
- **The Stag-Court's call on the Foxthaw evenings.** Roan does not hear
  it. The oilskin curtain over the loft window is not a defense; it is
  an instinct. Future writers MUST NOT have Roan *name* the Stag-Court,
  and MUST NOT confirm to him that the curtain matters. Maeve knows.
  Lyra suspects. Roan does not.
- **Lyra's salve.** Roan does not name Lyra in the warmed-dialogue
  acknowledgement line. He does not name the salve. He does not thank
  her. The whole arc is *unnen* and breaks if any of the three
  withholdings is lifted. Future writers wiring
  `roan_received_salve` MUST keep all three.
- **The black mare's name.** The black mare is named **Maeve.** Roan
  has never said this aloud to the human Maeve, and he never will.
  Future writers MUST NOT have Roan answer the question *what's the
  black mare's name* truthfully in front of Maeve. He is permitted, in
  warmed dialogue at high `dire_wolves` reduction, to tell the player
  alone, in the stable, with no one else present. *That* surfacing

## Author note for downstream agents

Roan's `default` line in `WorldBuilder.NPCS` is the line he gives to a
stranger who has just walked into the stable, and it should remain
short, hand-busy, and Steppe-flat. The *(adjusts a strap.)* parenthetical
is a verbal cue for **Polisher** to wire one of Roan's per-role idle
animations (THEME §12) on the same dialogue tick — buckle, curry-comb,
or saddle-lift. Drop the parenthetical from at most one of four warmed
variants — *only* the warmest, where the closeness has earned the
quieter bodily presence (compare Mara's *jingle, jingle* tic and Edda's
*\*hammer-clang\**).

The mood-keyed surface — once the matching JSON ships in
`eldoria-godot/data/dialogue/stablemaster_roan.json` — should mirror
`smith_edda.json`, `innkeeper_bram.json`, `herbalist_lyra.json`, and
`elder_maeve.json` in shape, with one structural difference: Roan's
warming channel was, per WORLD_STATE.md run 8, the *first* in the
village whose ONLY warm tier is `warm_faction_id: "dire_wolves"` /
`warm_faction_below: 0.5`. Once the wolf-bounty quest above ships,
Roan promotes to a fully 4-tier NPC — `warm_flag: "first_bounty_done"`
joins the existing faction tier — and the four `warm_lines` shapes
above are the canonical voice. Until the bounty ships, the four
faction-tier lines remain Roan's only warming.

The seasonal slot (Edda's `spring_first_warm_day`, Bram's
`honeysong_eve`, Mara's `sunpetal_first_morning`) is replaced for Roan
with **`reapmoon_meadow_hour`** — the hour Roan and Maeve do not walk
together to the meadow above the village, hat off, counting horses. The
line must NOT name Cailen, must NOT name the meadow, and must NOT name
Maeve. It is the quietest line in Roan's tree on purpose. *"Going up
to the meadow, traveler. Hour. Stable's open — ask the bay."* is a
workable shape. The *ask the bay* is the cue that the player is being
trusted with the stable, which is, for Roan, the largest welcome he
gives.

Roan's voice is the most spare in the village. He is a *small* presence
on a fast scene and a *fitting* presence on a slow one — he never
dominates, he never disappears. Builder should not lengthen his
sentences; the brevity is the character. Polisher should keep his hands
busy in every dialogue idle; the work is his speech. If a future writer
is tempted to give Roan a long monologue — please do not. The
withholding *is* the lore.
